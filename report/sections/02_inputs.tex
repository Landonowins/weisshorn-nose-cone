The entries of Table~\ref{tab:inputs} that drive the nose design are the following.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Maximum speed.} The Mach number $M = V_\infty/a$ compares the flight speed with the
speed of sound, the speed at which small pressure disturbances travel through the air (about
\SI{332}{m/s} at \SI{2}{km} in the ISA). With $M_{\max} \approx 1.4$, Weisshorn crosses the
\emph{transonic} range (roughly $0.8 < M < 1.2$, where subsonic and supersonic regions coexist
around the body) and flies a few seconds in the low supersonic range. The nose has to be designed
for this whole range, not for a single design Mach number.
\item \textbf{Engine.} The total impulse $I_t$ is the thrust integrated over the burn; the specific
impulse $I_{sp} = I_t/(m_p g_0)$ is the impulse delivered per unit weight of propellant. Together
they fix the propellant mass $m_p$, and with it the mass at burn-out that the nose design affects.
\item \textbf{Stability.} A rocket is statically stable if its centre of pressure (CP, the point
where the resultant aerodynamic normal force acts) lies behind its centre of gravity (CG): a
small angle of attack then creates a moment that turns the nose back into the wind. The
distance between the two, divided by the diameter, is the static margin in calibres (cal). The
nose moves the CP forward, so its shape affects the margin (Section~\ref{sec:criteria}).
\item \textbf{Payload.} A 3U CubeSat is a standard payload unit of $100\times100\times\SI{340.5}{mm}$.
It sits in the nose bay, so the shape of the nose decides how much of it fits inside the curved
part and how much extra cylinder (and vehicle length) is needed behind it.
\item \textbf{RF windows.} Carbon fibre conducts electricity and blocks radio signals. Antennas for
telemetry and GNSS therefore need a section of radio-transparent shell (glass fibre), and the nose
is a candidate location for it.
\item \textbf{Manufacturing heritage.} The Firehorn nose was laid up in prepreg (carbon fabric
already impregnated with epoxy, cured in an oven) on the outside of a 3D-printed male mould.
The laminate $[0/45/0]_{SO}$ is symmetric with an odd number of plies, i.e.\ five plies at
$0^\circ$, $45^\circ$, $0^\circ$, $45^\circ$ and $0^\circ$. The $0^\circ$ plies, aligned with the
axis, carry axial load and bending, and the $45^\circ$ plies carry shear and torsion. The tip was a separate printed part, and an aluminium coupler is bonded
inside the shoulder to connect the nose to the next bay.
\item \textbf{Thermal heritage.} The Firehorn requirement asks the nose to withstand an outer
surface temperature of \SI{390}{K} for \SI{5}{s}. Section~\ref{sec:heating} checks whether it
covers the faster, lighter Weisshorn.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What is assumed}

Everything below is an assumption of this study. Each one is chosen so that the comparison
between nose shapes is fair; none of them changes the ranking.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Trajectory.} Point mass, rail \SI{12}{m} at $84^\circ$, ISA, no wind, constant
\SI{4.5}{kN} for \SI{9.1}{s}. Propellant from $I_t/(I_{sp}g_0) = \SI{20.9}{kg}$, i.e.\ burn-out
mass \SI{54.1}{kg}. The drag of everything except the nose is scaled by a single factor
$k$ so that the baseline nose reaches the LVO apogee of \SI{9.0}{km}
(Section~\ref{sec:flight}). This gives $M_{\max}=1.47$, consistent with the LVO's Mach~1.4.
A point-mass model treats the vehicle as a particle acted on by thrust, drag and gravity and
ignores its rotation; for a statically stable rocket the axis stays aligned with the velocity,
and this is the standard model for apogee estimates. The ISA (International Standard Atmosphere)
gives temperature, pressure and density as functions of altitude. The factor $k$ is needed
because the drag of the rest of the vehicle is the least certain part of the model at this stage
(fins not designed, no flight data). Calibrating it once on the team's reference apogee means
that every nose is compared on a vehicle with the intended performance. Since all noses share the
same $k$, the ranking depends only on their differences, and Section~\ref{sec:robust} shows that
it does not change when the rest of the drag is varied.
\item \textbf{Thermal envelope.} The LVO masses ($75 \rightarrow \SI{45}{kg}$) on a
\SI{30}{\celsius} day give $M_{\max} \approx 1.56$. Heating is checked on this case. A lighter
vehicle reaches a higher speed, and hotter ambient air raises the stagnation temperature for a
given Mach number (Section~\ref{sec:heating_method}); together they give the hottest credible
flight.
\item \textbf{Fins} (not yet defined for Weisshorn): four trapezoidal fins, root \SI{300}{mm},
tip \SI{120}{mm}, span \SI{160}{mm}, sweep \SI{180}{mm}, \SI{5}{mm} hexagonal section. They only
enter the common drag and the CP shift; they do not change the ranking of the noses. Fins
provide most of the restoring normal force of the vehicle and therefore set where its CP lies.
\item \textbf{Payload envelope.} A 3U unit ($100\times100\times\SI{340.5}{mm}$) needs an outer
radius $r \geq \SI{77}{mm}$: \SI{70.7}{mm} half-diagonal ($100/\sqrt2$, the radius of the circle
through the corners of its square section), \SI{3}{mm} clearance, \SI{2}{mm}
guide, \SI{1.2}{mm} shell.
\item \textbf{Shell.} \SI{1.2}{mm} CFRP at the Firehorn as-built areal density
(\SI{0.98}{kg/m^2}, the mass per unit of wetted surface), plus a \SI{60}{g} machined aluminium tip.
\item \textbf{Transonic nose wave drag} ($0.8 < M < 1.1$): smooth rise from zero at $M = 0.8$
to the computed $M = 1.1$ value at $M = 1.0$ (Section~\ref{sec:transonic}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What could not be found}
\label{sec:flags}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Alex Marseille's nose cone report} is not on the ERT Drive or the wiki. The searches
covered ``Marseille'', ``nose cone'', ``nosecone'', ``ogive'', ``SSR'' and ``Farewell''. His
documented work is the CFD of the \SI{54}{mm} supersonic semester rocket (SSR weekly meetings
01--12, spring 2026; report on Overleaf, not shared in the Drive). As CFD advisor he set the
semester goal ``WS (Cd, Nosecone)'' (2027\_C\_FD\_TM\_3, 01.10.2026), which is this study. The
heritage used instead is the 2021 supersonic nosecone semester project (materials and tip,
ERT Drive, \emph{Report} folder), the Wildhorn
optimisation note (2022\_FD\_TN\_C01) and the Firehorn nosecone DJF/DDF.
\item \textbf{No Weisshorn nosecone requirement} (length, mass, thermal) exists yet. The four
Weisshorn nosecone pages (DJF, DDF, FEAR, tooling DJF) are empty templates. The nosecone
requirements of the 2027 database cover only the Aletsch test rocket.
\item \textbf{Weisshorn fins, CG and mass breakdown} are not defined (FD page is a template).
The static margin is therefore given as a \emph{change} relative to the baseline nose.
\item \textbf{No supersonic flight data} exists at ERT. Firehorn~1 reached \SI{223}{m}, so there
is no measured nose heating or drag to calibrate against.
\end{itemize}
