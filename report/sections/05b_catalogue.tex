\section{Design Catalogue}
\label{sec:catalogue}

This section presents the seven candidate nose cones as designs at the recommended length,
$L/D = 4.5$ ($L = \SI{810}{mm}$, $D = \SI{180}{mm}$), each with the \SI{60}{mm} bonded shoulder of
the Firehorn design. Figure~\ref{fig:gallery} shows them to scale with the 3U payload placed as far
forward as each profile allows, and Figure~\ref{fig:fields} shows the computed flow around each of
them at $M = 1.4$. Table~\ref{tab:catalogue} collects all the numbers. The wave-drag values in this
section come from Euler runs made directly at $L/D = 4.5$; they agree with the values interpolated
from $L/D = 3$, 4, 5 to within 0.3\,\% (Section~\ref{sec:check45}). Watertight STL solids of every
design are in \texttt{results/cad/} (Appendix~\ref{app:scripts}).
The catalogue turns the trade study into concrete hardware: for each shape it shows the outer
mould line that would be built, where the payload sits in it, and the flow it produces.

In Figure~\ref{fig:gallery}, the further forward the dashed 3U box can be pushed, the shorter the
payload-bay cylinder behind the shoulder (dotted extension). In Figure~\ref{fig:fields}, the red
region ahead of each nose is the air compressed between the bow shock and the body; its outer
edge, the shock, leans back at roughly the Mach angle ($45.6^\circ$ at $M = 1.4$,
Equation~\eqref{eq:mach}). The blue regions are expansions. On the cone and the power noses they
are concentrated in a fan at the shoulder kink; on the tangent profiles they are spread over the
aft part of the nose itself. The more intense the red region in contact with the nose surface,
and the less blue on it, the higher the wave drag.

Table~\ref{tab:catalogue} groups the metrics in three blocks: geometry (volume, wetted area,
angles, centre of pressure), aerodynamics and flight (wave drag, apogee change at three lengths),
and integration (mass, payload room, bay length, static margin). The datasheets below discuss
each design in the same order.

\subsection{Conical}

\textbf{Geometry.} A straight generator, $6.3^\circ$ semi-angle at $L/D=4.5$. It has the smallest
volume (\SI{6.9}{L}) and wetted area (\SI{0.230}{m^2}) of all designs, hence the lightest shell
(\SI{286}{g}), but it meets the cylinder with a $6.3^\circ$ kink.

\textbf{Flow.} The conical-flow pressure ($C_p \approx 0.06$ at $M=1.4$) acts on the whole surface
without relaxing. All the turning happens at once in a strong expansion fan at the shoulder
(Figure~\ref{fig:fields}). That is why it has the highest wave drag at every Mach number, 46\,\%
above Von K\'arm\'an at $M=1.4$, and why its drag climbs steeply towards Mach 1. As $M$
decreases, the conical shock strengthens and moves towards detachment, so the uniform surface
pressure rises (Figure~\ref{fig:validation}a).

\textbf{Integration and manufacture.} Only \SI{117}{mm} of the 3U fits inside the cone, so the nose
bay grows to \SI{1043}{mm}. The mould is the simplest of all (a developable surface, i.e.\ one that can be unrolled
onto a plane without stretching, so it can even be laid up from flat patterns), but the shoulder kink needs a fillet at the coupler joint.

\textbf{Verdict: rejected.} It is the worst on drag ($-\SI{132}{m}$ against Von K\'arm\'an), payload
room and bay length. It also has the most aft centre of pressure ($x_{cp}/L = 2/3$), which is
helpful for stability but irrelevant here because the margin changes by only $+0.09$ cal.
(An aft nose CP moves the vehicle CP back, which increases the static margin.)

\subsection{Tangent ogive}

\textbf{Geometry.} A circular arc tangent to the body, the classic subsonic nose used on
Bella Lui~II and Nordend. It has a $13^\circ$ tip semi-angle, \SI{11.0}{L} of volume and a tangent
shoulder. Its sharp apex has a finite angle, so near the tip it behaves like a $13^\circ$ cone,
about twice the semi-angle of the $6.3^\circ$ cone of the same length.

\textbf{Flow.} The fuller forebody turns the flow harder near the tip (stronger compression than
Von K\'arm\'an), then expands continuously; the wall pressure goes below ambient over the aft
40\,\%. Wave drag is 21\,\% above Von K\'arm\'an at $M=1.4$.

\textbf{Integration and manufacture.} It is the best of the low-drag families for packaging:
\SI{309}{mm} of the 3U fits in the curved part, giving a \SI{870}{mm} nose bay. The constant
curvature makes it the easiest smooth profile to template or machine: a single radius describes
the whole generator.

\textbf{Verdict: acceptable alternative for volume-driven designs.} It costs \SI{79}{m} of apogee
against Von K\'arm\'an, about \SI{0.55}{kg} of dry-mass margin, for \SI{37}{mm} less nose bay.

\subsection{Parabolic ($K' = 1$)}

\textbf{Geometry and flow.} At the same length the parabola is practically the tangent ogive. The
volume, wetted area, wave drag and pressure field are all the same (wave drag within 0.4\,\%), and its
shoulder is also tangent.

\textbf{Verdict: same as the ogive.} It is listed for completeness: it is
the ``parabola'' that Crowell's chart rates best in the high subsonic range (Table~\ref{tab:crowell},
Section~\ref{sec:crowell}),
which is irrelevant for a vehicle whose drag losses are concentrated at $M = 0.8$--1.5.

\subsection{Power series $n = \tfrac12$ (Firehorn)}

\textbf{Geometry.} $\eta = \xi^{1/2}$ starts with a vertical tangent: the tip is blunt by
construction. It has the same volume as Von K\'arm\'an (\SI{10.3}{L}), but the volume sits
further forward, and it meets the cylinder with a $3.2^\circ$ kink.

\textbf{Flow.} The blunt apex detaches the bow shock and creates a small subsonic pocket (the only
closed sonic line in Figure~\ref{fig:fields}). The sonic line encloses the region where the flow
behind the normal part of the shock has been slowed below Mach 1; the pressure there is close to
the stagnation pressure, which gives the spike of Figure~\ref{fig:cp}. The overpressure is a short, intense spike at the
apex; behind it the wall pressure stays slightly above ambient all the way to the shoulder, where
the kink produces an expansion. The net wave drag is only 3--10\,\% above Von K\'arm\'an at
$L/D=4.5$. On short noses ($L/D = 3$), and at $M \leq 1.1$ for $L/D = 4$, it is the lowest of all. Its
drag is concentrated on the small frontal area of the blunt apex, whereas the rest of the nose is
very slender and carries almost no overpressure. On a short nose the tangent profiles have to
turn the flow harder over their whole length, and this trade-off favours the power-$\tfrac12$
shape.
Wildhorn chose this shape in 2022 on the basis of OpenRocket simulations.

\textbf{Integration and manufacture.} \SI{217}{mm} of the 3U fits inside (nose bay
\SI{943}{mm}). It is built like the other profiles (prepreg on a PET-CF male mould, removable
tip), and the blunt apex naturally ends on a tip insert.

\textbf{Verdict: the choice for a short nose.} At $L/D=4.5$ it is \SI{22}{m} below Von K\'arm\'an
(about \SI{0.15}{kg}) and within \SI{31}{m} of it in every robustness scenario
(Section~\ref{sec:robust}), but it has a shoulder kink and a \SI{36}{mm} longer nose bay. If the
nose has to be short ($L/D \leq 3.5$), it has the lowest wave drag of all profiles
(Table~\ref{tab:alternatives}).

\subsection{Power series $n = \tfrac34$}

\textbf{Geometry.} It lies between the cone and the $\tfrac12$-power nose: a sharp-ish tip ($74^\circ$
apex tangent over a very short distance; the slope is infinite at the apex but the radius of
curvature is zero, so the tip is pointed), a slender body, and the second-smallest volume
(\SI{8.2}{L}) and wetted area. It has a $4.8^\circ$ shoulder kink.

\textbf{Flow.} Its wave drag falls fastest with Mach number ($0.0513 \rightarrow 0.0432$ between
$M = 1.2$ and 1.6). Its slender, nearly conical body behaves like a cone whose shock weakens
quickly as the Mach number rises, while its pointed tip avoids the stagnation spike of the
power-$\tfrac12$ nose. Crowell's chart rates it among the best around $M \approx 1.5$--1.9. Its
low wetted area saves friction, so over the Weisshorn flight it ends \SI{18}{m} below
Von K\'arm\'an at $L/D = 4.5$.

\textbf{Integration.} It has the worst packaging of the smooth shapes: \SI{152}{mm} of the 3U
inside, a \SI{1008}{mm} nose bay and a kinked shoulder.

\textbf{Verdict: not selected.} Its drag is competitive, but it would only make sense on a
faster vehicle ($M_{\max} \approx 2$) with a payload outside the nose.

\subsection{LV-Haack ($C = \tfrac13$)}

\textbf{Geometry.} The Haack nose that minimises wave drag for a given length \emph{and volume}. It
is the fullest design (\SI{11.6}{L}), with a tangent shoulder. It is the natural choice when the
internal volume, rather than the base diameter, is fixed by the payload.

\textbf{Flow.} It is close to the ogive, with a stronger compression at the apex. Its wave drag is
13--19\,\% above Von K\'arm\'an over $M = 1.2$--1.6.

\textbf{Integration.} It has the best payload room: \SI{320}{mm} of the 3U inside, a \SI{870}{mm}
nose bay, and the heaviest shell (\SI{376}{g}). It also has the most forward centre of pressure
($-0.08$ cal), the largest reduction of the static margin among the candidates.

\textbf{Verdict: the choice if payload volume were the driver.} It costs \SI{69}{m} against
Von K\'arm\'an for the same bay length as the ogive, so between the two volume-optimised options
it is marginally better than the ogive.

\subsection{Von K\'arm\'an ($C = 0$, recommended)}

\textbf{Geometry.} The Haack nose that minimises wave drag for a given length \emph{and base area}.
Its apex has an infinite slope but a vanishing radius ($y \propto x^{3/4}$ near the tip), so in
practice it is a slender needle that thickens quickly. Unlike the LV-Haack nose it does not
constrain the volume, so it can stay thinner near the tip and spread the area growth more evenly
along the length, which is what lowers its wave drag. It has \SI{10.3}{L} of volume, a tangent
shoulder and $x_{cp}/L = 0.5$.

\textbf{Flow.} The compression is weak and concentrated in the first few per cent of the length.
The wall pressure then decreases monotonically and is below ambient over the aft third
(Figure~\ref{fig:cp}), which partly cancels the forward overpressure. It has the lowest wave drag
of the seven at every computed Mach number at $L/D = 4.5$ (0.0437, 0.0425 and 0.0414 at
$M = 1.2$, 1.4 and 1.6). The \SI{4.5}{mm} spherical tip replaces the needle apex by a
stagnation region a few millimetres across (as on any blunt tip), at a cost of $+0.5$ to $+1.4\,\%$
in wave drag (direct runs at $L/D = 4.5$).

\textbf{Integration and manufacture.} \SI{254}{mm} of the 3U fits inside the curved part (nose bay
\SI{907}{mm}). The tangent shoulder gives a clean bond line for the Al coupler. The profile is
generated from one closed-form equation (CSV/STL provided).

\textbf{Verdict: recommended.} It gives the highest apogee at $L/D = 4.5$ in all seven robustness
scenarios, has no shoulder kink and gives reasonable payload room.

\subsection{Literature cross-check}
\label{sec:crowell}

The drag-rating chart compiled by Crowell (reproduced on Wikipedia's ``Nose cone design'' page,
CC BY-SA 4.0, and used in the Firehorn nosecone DJF) ranks the shapes from 1 (superior) to 4
(inferior) against Mach number. It condenses published drag comparisons into four qualitative
classes and is the reference the Firehorn DJF used to choose its nose; it therefore provides an
independent, if coarse, check of the computed ranking. Table~\ref{tab:crowell} lists the ratings read from the chart at
five Mach numbers. The chart does not state the fineness ratio of the data behind it.

The two sources agree on what matters most: the ogive and the cone are the worst choices at
transonic and low supersonic speeds, and the Von K\'arm\'an, Haack and power-law noses are the
good ones. They differ around $M \approx 1.5$, where the chart prefers the $\tfrac12$-power nose
to Von K\'arm\'an. The Euler results reproduce that preference at $L/D = 3$ (power $\tfrac12$
ahead) but not at $L/D \geq 4$, where Von K\'arm\'an is lower at every Mach number. The
ranking depends on fineness, and the chart is a coarse, fineness-free summary. The
recommendation therefore rests on the computed ranking at the actual design fineness, which is
also robust to the modelling assumptions (Section~\ref{sec:robust}).
