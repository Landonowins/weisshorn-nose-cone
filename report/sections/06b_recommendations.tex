\subsection{Recommended design}

\noindent\fbox{\parbox{\dimexpr\textwidth-2\fboxsep-2\fboxrule}{%
\textbf{Von K\'arm\'an nose cone, $L = \SI{810}{mm}$ ($L/D = 4.5$), $D = \SI{180}{mm}$, tangent
\SI{60}{mm} shoulder, \SI{4.5}{mm} spherical Al 6082-T6 tip insert, CFRP $[0/45/0]_{SO}$ shell
(\SI{1.2}{mm}).}\\[3pt]
Predicted: $C_{D,\rm w} = \FINCDC$ at $M = 1.4$ (lowest of seven profiles at every computed Mach
number); \FINDAPO{} of apogee against the LVS-length baseline; \FINMASS{} for shell and tip;
\FINTHREEU{} of the 3U inside the curved part; tip below \TIPAL{} and shell inner face below
\SHELLENV{} on a \SI{30}{\celsius} day.}}

Owners are the ERT teams (Nomenclature); PDR and CDR are the preliminary and critical design
reviews. R6 asks for a viscous check with RANS and the $k$-$\omega$ SST turbulence model, a
two-equation model widely used for external aerodynamics with adverse pressure gradients and
shock--boundary-layer interaction. It covers the transonic range, where the Euler model of this
report does not apply (Section~\ref{sec:transonic}), and gives the normal force at incidence,
which Barrowman's method only estimates. R7 transfers the results to RocketPy, the team's
six-degree-of-freedom trajectory simulator, which also models the rotation of the vehicle.

\subsection{When to choose another design}

The recommendation assumes the current Weisshorn priorities: apogee margin, a 3U payload in the
nose bay, and a length cap. If those priorities change, Table~\ref{tab:alternatives} gives the
design to use instead.

Each row follows from the results above. A shorter vehicle keeps the same profile and gives up
the length gain of Section~\ref{sec:selection}. The power-$\tfrac12$ nose is the closest
alternative in performance and the only one with an existing process. If the payload volume
becomes the driver, the LV-Haack nose, the minimum-drag shape for a given volume, houses the most
payload for the smallest drag penalty. On short noses the power-$\tfrac12$ shape has the lowest
wave drag (Section~\ref{sec:trade}). At higher Mach numbers the wave drag of the power-$\tfrac34$
nose falls fastest (Section~\ref{sec:catalogue}), so the ranking has to be recomputed. Below
$M = 0.8$ there is no wave drag, and the choice is made on volume and manufacturing alone.

\subsection{What not to do}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Do not use a cone or a tangent ogive} for a supersonic Weisshorn. They lose
\SIrange{79}{132}{m} at the same length, and both this study and the literature rate them worst in
the transonic and low supersonic range.
\item \textbf{Do not use a printed thermoplastic tip.} Its surface follows the stagnation temperature,
which reaches \SI{156}{\celsius} on a hot day.
\item \textbf{Do not size the nose by fineness alone.} A longer nose pays only if the extra length
also houses payload. Measure it as nose-bay length, which is what the \SI{5.4}{m} cap actually
constrains.
\end{itemize}
