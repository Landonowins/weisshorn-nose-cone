\section{Design Options}
\label{sec:options}

The nose shape matters for Weisshorn because of the way air flows around a body above Mach 1.
This section first summarises that physics (Section~\ref{sec:physics}), then defines the candidate
profiles (Section~\ref{sec:profiles}) and the criteria on which they are compared
(Section~\ref{sec:criteria}).

\subsection{Supersonic flow over a nose and wave drag}
\label{sec:physics}

\paragraph{Subsonic and supersonic flow.} In subsonic flow, the pressure disturbances created by the
body travel upstream faster than the body moves. The oncoming air therefore starts to move aside
well ahead of the nose and the streamlines adjust smoothly. As long as the flow stays attached,
the overpressure on the front of a streamlined nose is largely recovered on its aft part, so its
drag is mostly skin friction. Above $M = 1$, disturbances can no longer travel upstream: a small
disturbance created at a point only influences the region inside a downstream cone of half-angle
\begin{equation}
\mu = \arcsin\frac{1}{M},
\label{eq:mach}
\end{equation}
the \emph{Mach angle} ($\mu = 45.6^\circ$ at $M = 1.4$). The air ahead of the nose receives no
advance signal and is deflected abruptly through a \emph{shock wave}, a thin layer across which
pressure, density and temperature jump and the velocity drops. Entropy increases across a shock:
part of the kinetic energy of the flow is dissipated irreversibly. Where the surface curves back
towards the axial direction, the flow turns through a continuous \emph{expansion} (a
Prandtl--Meyer expansion), in which the pressure decreases, possibly below the ambient value.

\paragraph{Wave drag.} The pressure on a supersonic nose is therefore above ambient near the tip
and decreases along the nose. The nose surface faces forward everywhere, so this overpressure
gives a net axial force, the \emph{wave drag}. It exists even in an inviscid fluid. Physically it
is the energy carried away by the waves and dissipated in the shock. It is expressed as the
coefficient $C_{D,\rm w} = D_{\rm w}/(q_\infty A_b)$, where $D_{\rm w}$ is the wave-drag force,
$q_\infty = \tfrac12\rho_\infty V_\infty^2$ the free-stream dynamic pressure and $A_b = \pi R^2$ the
base area (Equation~\eqref{eq:cdw}). Wave drag appears once the local flow becomes sonic somewhere
on the nose, around $M \approx 0.8$ for slender bodies. It rises steeply to a peak near $M = 1$
and decreases slowly in the supersonic range. Of the drag components of a rocket, it is the only
one controlled by the nose shape: skin friction depends on the wetted area, and base drag on the
aft end (Section~\ref{sec:flight}).

\paragraph{Attached and detached shocks.} A sharp, slender tip deflects the flow by a small angle,
so the shock is weak, oblique and attached to the tip. If the tip is blunt, or its half-angle too
large for the Mach number, no attached shock exists. The shock then detaches and stands ahead of
the body as a curved \emph{bow shock}. Its central part is a normal shock, behind which the flow is
subsonic and the pressure rises almost to the stagnation pressure. This is why supersonic noses
are pointed, and why any rounding of the tip has to stay small (Section~\ref{sec:bluntness}).

\paragraph{Slender-body theory.} For a slender nose ($L/D \gg 1$) the disturbances are small and the
compressible potential-flow equation can be linearised. In this theory the wave drag depends
only on the axial distribution of cross-sectional area $S(x) = \pi y(x)^2$, through its second
derivative $S''(x)$, i.e.\ through how abruptly area is added along the nose. Two results follow.
First, within a profile family all radii scale with $R$ and all lengths with $L$, so
$S'' \propto R^2/L^2$ and
\begin{equation}
C_{D,\rm w} \propto \left(\frac{R}{L}\right)^2 \propto \frac{1}{(L/D)^2} :
\label{eq:fineness}
\end{equation}
doubling the length of a nose divides its wave drag by four. Second, the area distribution of
minimum wave drag can be found analytically. For a given length and base area it is the
Von K\'arm\'an profile, with $C_{D,\rm w} = 1/(L/D)^2$. For a given length and volume it is the
LV-Haack profile (Section~\ref{sec:profiles}). The theory assumes small disturbances and replaces
shocks by Mach waves, so it gives trends rather than exact values, and it is least accurate near
$M = 1$. The Euler computations of Section~\ref{sec:method} provide the values used in this study.

\subsection{Candidate profiles}
\label{sec:profiles}

The nose is defined by three choices: the \textbf{profile family}, the \textbf{fineness}
$L/D$ (curved length over base diameter) and the \textbf{tip bluntness} $r_n/R$. Seven
profiles are compared (Table~\ref{tab:profiles}, Figure~\ref{fig:profiles}); Figure~\ref{fig:schematics}
sketches each of them and how it is constructed. With $\xi = x/L$ (the
axial coordinate from the apex, normalised by the length) and $\eta = y/R$ (the local radius,
normalised by the base radius), they are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Conical}, $\eta = \xi$: a straight generator of half-angle $\arctan(R/L)$
($7.1^\circ$ at $L/D = 4$). It is the simplest shape, but it meets the body tube with a kink equal
to its half-angle.
\item \textbf{Tangent ogive}: the generator is a circular arc of radius $\rho = (R^2+L^2)/(2R)$ whose
centre lies in the base plane, so that the arc is tangent to the body tube at the shoulder. It is
the traditional subsonic rocket nose and the shape of earlier ERT vehicles.
\item \textbf{Parabolic series}, $\eta = (2\xi - K'\xi^2)/(2-K')$; the case $K' = 1$ used here is the
parabola that is tangent to the body at the shoulder.
\item \textbf{Power series}, $\eta = \xi^n$ with $0 < n < 1$ ($n = 1$ is the cone). The smaller $n$,
the fuller the front of the nose. For $n<1$ the slope $\mathrm d\eta/\mathrm d\xi = n\xi^{n-1}$ is
infinite at the apex. For $n = \tfrac12$ the profile is a parabola about the axis, $y^2 = R^2x/L$,
whose apex has a radius of curvature $R^2/(2L)$ (\SI{5.6}{mm} at $L/D = 4$): this nose is blunt by
construction. At the shoulder the slope is $n R/L \neq 0$, so the power noses meet the body with
a kink $\theta_{\rm sh} = \arctan(nR/L)$.
\item \textbf{Haack series}: the area distributions of minimum wave drag from slender-body theory.
With the variable $\theta = \arccos(1-2\xi)$, which runs from 0 at the apex to $\pi$ at the base,
\begin{equation*}
\eta = \frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\sqrt{\theta - \tfrac12\sin 2\theta + C\sin^3\theta} .
\end{equation*}
The parameter $C$ selects
the constraint: $C = 0$ gives the minimum wave drag for a given length and base area
(\textbf{Von K\'arm\'an}), $C = \tfrac13$ the minimum for a given length and volume
(\textbf{LV-Haack}). Both are tangent to the body at the shoulder. Near the apex the
Von K\'arm\'an profile grows as $\eta \propto \xi^{3/4}$, with a vertical tangent but a zero tip
radius.
\end{itemize}

At equal length the profiles differ mainly in how quickly they gain radius
(Figure~\ref{fig:profiles}a). The power-$\tfrac12$ nose is the fullest over the first fifth of the
length. The ogive, parabola and LV-Haack are the fullest over the middle and aft part, and the cone
is the thinnest everywhere. Each curve's length above the dotted line $r = \SI{77}{mm}$ is the
part of the nose that can house the 3U payload. In Figure~\ref{fig:profiles}b the apex of the
Von K\'arm\'an nose is replaced by a \SI{4.5}{mm} sphere; the change is confined to the first
\SI{10}{mm} of the nose.

Shapes discarded before computing: the \emph{elliptical} nose (good only subsonically; its blunt
front gives a strong bow shock above Mach 1) and the \emph{secant ogive} (no advantage over the
tangent ogive and LV-Haack at the same volume, and a slope discontinuity at the shoulder). The
elliptical nose is a quarter-ellipse whose front is a rounded, blunt face; above Mach 1 it carries a
detached bow shock with the near-stagnation pressure described in Section~\ref{sec:physics}. The
secant ogive is a circular arc of larger radius than the tangent ogive, cut so that it meets the
body at an angle.

\subsection{Comparison criteria}
\label{sec:criteria}

What matters for the comparison:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Wave drag.} At Weisshorn's Mach numbers (up to 1.5) the nose pressure drag
depends mostly on $L/D$ and on how much area the profile adds near the tip. Slender-body theory
gives the Von K\'arm\'an profile the minimum wave drag for given length and base area,
$C_{D,\rm w} = 1/(L/D)^2$. This makes the Von K\'arm\'an nose the theoretical favourite. Whether
this linear-theory result survives at Weisshorn's finite thickness and Mach numbers is what the
Euler computations of Section~\ref{sec:results} check.
\item \textbf{Volume and payload.} Fuller profiles (ogive, parabolic, LV-Haack) reach
$r=\SI{77}{mm}$ further forward, so more of the 3U unit sits inside the curved part. The part of
the 3U that does not fit inside the curved nose needs a cylindrical extension of the nose bay,
which adds vehicle length within the \SI{5.4}{m} cap.
\item \textbf{Stability.} The nose carries $C_{N\alpha}=2$ whatever its shape. Its centre of
pressure sits at $x_{cp}=L-V/A_b$, so a fuller nose moves the vehicle CP forward. At a small angle
of attack $\alpha$, each part of the vehicle produces a force normal to its axis,
$N = q_\infty A_b C_{N\alpha}\,\alpha$. Slender-body theory gives the same slope
$C_{N\alpha} = 2$ per radian for any nose that closes on the base area $A_b$, because the normal
force generated by a section is proportional to its rate of change of area $\mathrm dS/\mathrm dx$,
and its integral over the nose only depends on the base area. The shape only sets where the force
acts, $x_{cp} = L - V/A_b$ from the apex ($2L/3$ for a cone). The vehicle CP is the
$C_{N\alpha}$-weighted average of the nose and fin contributions, and the static margin is
$(x_{cp} - x_{cg})/D$; moving the CP forward reduces it.
\item \textbf{Manufacture.} A tangent shoulder ($\theta_{\rm sh}=0$) avoids a kink at the coupler
joint. A sharp tip cannot be laid up in prepreg (ply overlaps stack at the apex), so every option
uses a separate machined tip. A kink would also create a local expansion in the flow, need a
fillet in the mould, and put the bond line of the coupler at a corner of the shell.
\end{itemize}
