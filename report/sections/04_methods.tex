\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

The chain is: Euler wave drag of each nose $\rightarrow$ vehicle drag build-up $\rightarrow$ ascent
trajectory $\rightarrow$ apogee, heating, stability. All scripts are in \texttt{scripts/} (Appendix~\ref{app:scripts}).
The wave drag is the only shape-dependent quantity, but a drag coefficient alone does not say how
much a shape is worth. It is therefore converted into apogee with a flight model, which also
accounts for the length and mass of each nose, and the same trajectories drive the heating
assessment.

\subsection{Nose wave drag: axisymmetric Euler solver}
\label{sec:euler}

Above Mach 1 the nose pressure drag is an inviscid effect (shock and expansion), so the
axisymmetric Euler equations are solved for each nose followed by a $5R$ cylinder. The Euler
equations express the conservation of mass, momentum and energy of a compressible fluid without
viscosity and heat conduction, i.e.\ the Navier--Stokes equations without their viscous terms.
Neglecting viscosity is appropriate for the wave drag, which is set by the pressure field of the
outer flow. The boundary layer is thin at these Reynolds numbers (about $2\times10^7$ on the nose
length at $M_{\max}$), and its main effect, skin friction, is added separately in the flight
model (Section~\ref{sec:flight}). At zero angle of attack the flow is \emph{axisymmetric}: it is
the same in every plane through the axis, so only the half-plane $(x, r)$ is computed. In
finite-volume form, for one cell and per radian of azimuth,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial (\boldsymbol U V)}{\partial t} + \sum_{\rm faces} \boldsymbol F\cdot\boldsymbol n\, \ell\, r_f
= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & p\,A & 0 \end{pmatrix}^{\!\top},
\qquad V = A\, r_c ,
\label{eq:euler}
\end{equation}
with $\boldsymbol U = (\rho,\rho u,\rho v,E)$ per radian. $\boldsymbol U$ is the vector of
conserved variables: density, axial and radial momentum per unit volume, and total energy per unit
volume. $\boldsymbol F$ is the corresponding flux, the rate at which each quantity crosses a face,
including the pressure force acting on it. $\boldsymbol n$ is the outward unit normal of a face,
$\ell$ its length in the $(x,r)$ plane and $r_f$ the radius of its midpoint, so $\ell\,r_f$ is
the face area per radian. $A$ is the area of the cell in the $(x,r)$ plane and $r_c$ the radius of
its centroid, so $V = A r_c$ is the volume per radian of the ring-shaped cell. The equation states
that the content of a cell changes by the net inflow through its faces plus a source. The source
$p\,A$ appears in the radial momentum equation only. The two azimuthal faces of a ring-shaped cell
are not parallel, so the pressure acting on them has a net outward radial component, which has no
equivalent in planar 2D flow.

The scheme is a cell-centred
finite-volume method with HLLC fluxes, MUSCL reconstruction of primitive variables (minmod),
SSP-RK2 and local time stepping (CFL 0.85). Writing the volume as $A r_c$ preserves a uniform
flow exactly. In detail:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
\item \emph{Finite volume}: the unknowns are cell averages of $\boldsymbol U$, updated with the fluxes
through the faces. Mass, momentum and energy are conserved exactly, which places shocks at the
right position.
\item \emph{HLLC flux}: at each face, the two neighbouring gas states form a local Riemann problem (a
jump between two states), whose solution is a set of waves travelling at speeds related to $u-a$,
$u$ and $u+a$. The HLLC solver approximates this pattern with three waves and returns the flux
through the face. It resolves shocks within two to three cells without oscillations.
\item \emph{MUSCL with minmod}: inside each cell the variables are reconstructed as linear functions,
which makes the scheme second-order accurate in space. The minmod limiter flattens these slopes
near discontinuities, so that no spurious oscillations appear at shocks.
\item \emph{SSP-RK2 and local time stepping}: a two-stage Runge--Kutta integration that keeps this
non-oscillatory property. Only the steady state matters, so each cell advances with its own
largest stable time step. The CFL number is the ratio of that time step to the time a wave needs
to cross the cell; an explicit scheme is stable only for values below one.
\end{itemize}

The grid is body-fitted ($160\times80$ cells), starts on the stagnation streamline
upstream of the tip, keeps near-wall cells orthogonal to the body and clusters points at the tip
and at the shoulder. The outer boundary is a hyperbola outside the bow shock with
characteristic (Riemann-invariant) far-field conditions; the wall is a slip wall and the axis a
symmetry line (Figure~\ref{fig:grid}). A body-fitted grid has one family of grid lines along the
wall, so the wall condition is applied exactly. The outer boundary must lie upstream of the bow
shock so that the flow entering the domain is the undisturbed free stream. The characteristic
conditions impose free-stream values on the waves entering the domain and let outgoing waves
leave without reflection. In inviscid flow the only condition at a solid wall is that no fluid
crosses it; the velocity is free to slip along it.

The drag coefficient is the integral of the wall pressure over the nose,
\begin{equation}
C_{D,\rm w} = \frac{1}{q_\infty \pi R^2}\int_{\rm nose} (p-p_\infty)\,(-n_x)\, 2\pi r\,\mathrm ds ,
\label{eq:cdw}
\end{equation}
and a run is converged when $C_{D,\rm w}$ changes by less than $2\times10^{-5}$ (relative) over
250 iterations. In Equation~\eqref{eq:cdw}, $p - p_\infty$ is the wall overpressure, $n_x$ the
axial component of the outward wall normal (negative on a forward-facing surface, so
$-n_x > 0$), $2\pi r\,\mathrm ds$ the area of a surface ring of width $\mathrm ds$ at radius $r$,
and $q_\infty\pi R^2$ the reference force. Only the pressure difference matters, since a uniform
pressure gives no net force on a closed body. The convergence threshold is far below the
differences between shapes. The Mach numbers are computed from high to low (1.6, 1.4, 1.2, 1.1; also 1.3 for
the cone and ogive), each started from the previous solution. Starting from a converged
neighbouring solution shortens the transient, because the shock system only has to move, not
form. Convergence slows as the Mach number decreases: the bow shock stands further ahead and the
subsonic region behind it grows, and in a subsonic region disturbances propagate in all directions
instead of being swept out of the domain. The $M=1.1$ runs, with a large
subsonic region behind the bow shock, were stopped at 6000 iterations before meeting this
criterion. They only set the end point of the transonic model (Section~\ref{sec:transonic}) and
carry a larger uncertainty, estimated at 1--2\,\%. Parabolic and power-$\tfrac34$ were computed at
$L/D=4$ only and scaled with $1/(L/D)^2$; LV-Haack and the ogive at $L/D=5$ were stopped at $M=1.2$.

\textbf{Validation.} For a sharp cone the surface pressure is uniform and $C_{D,\rm w}=C_p$, which
is known exactly from the Taylor--Maccoll equation. For a sharp cone at zero incidence the shock is
a straight conical surface attached to the apex, and the flow between shock and cone is
\emph{conical}: every property is constant along rays from the apex. The Euler equations then
reduce to an ordinary differential equation in the ray angle, the Taylor--Maccoll equation, which
is integrated numerically to high accuracy (\texttt{scripts/taylor\_maccoll.py}). Because the
overpressure is uniform, Equation~\eqref{eq:cdw} reduces to $C_{D,\rm w} = C_p$. Figure~\ref{fig:validation}a compares the
two for four cone angles (7.1$^\circ$ to 20$^\circ$, $M = 1.1$--2): the largest error is
0.46\,\%. The $20^\circ$ cone at $M=1.2$ has a detached shock and no conical solution: its
half-angle is larger than the largest deflection an attached shock can produce at that Mach
number. The grid convergence
study (Figure~\ref{fig:validation}b) is done on the Von K\'arm\'an nose at $L/D=4$. At $M=1.4$
it uses three grids ($80\times40$, $160\times80$ production, $320\times160$), the observed order
$p$ and the GCI of Roache ($F_s = 1.25$). At $M=1.2$ the fine run did not finish in time, so the
two-grid estimate is used: formal order $p=2$ and $F_s=3$.

The grid study quantifies the discretisation error. With the fine, production and coarse results
$f_1$, $f_2$, $f_3$ and a refinement ratio $r = 2$ in each direction,
\begin{equation}
p = \frac{\ln\left[(f_3-f_2)/(f_2-f_1)\right]}{\ln r}, \qquad
f_\infty = f_1 + \frac{f_1-f_2}{r^p-1}, \qquad
\mathrm{GCI}_2 = F_s\left|\frac{f_2-f_1}{f_2}\right|\frac{r^p}{r^p-1} .
\label{eq:gci}
\end{equation}
The observed order $p$ is the rate at which the error falls with the cell size. It should match the
design order of the scheme (2), which shows that the grids are fine enough for the error to
behave asymptotically. $f_\infty$ is the Richardson-extrapolated, grid-independent value.
$\mathrm{GCI}_2$ is a conservative error band on the production-grid result, with the safety factor
$F_s$. With two grids only, the band is $F_s|(f_3-f_2)/f_2|/(r^p-1)$, with the formal order
$p = 2$ and the larger $F_s = 3$.

Taylor--Maccoll solution (lines). (b) Grid convergence of the nose wave drag, normalised by the

Figure~\ref{fig:validation}a shows that the solver reproduces the exact cone pressures over the
whole range of angles and Mach numbers, including the steep rise as the shock approaches
detachment. Figure~\ref{fig:validation}b gives $p = 2.0$ and a GCI of 0.5\,\% at $M = 1.4$ (1.1\,\%
at $M = 1.2$ with the two-grid estimate). The production grid is therefore accurate to about
0.5--1\,\%, well below the 3--46\,\% by which the other shapes exceed the Von K\'arm\'an wave drag
at $L/D = 4.5$ (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\subsection{Transonic range}
\label{sec:transonic}

Steady Euler solutions converge too slowly below $M=1.1$ (detached shock far upstream, large
subsonic region), and RANS is needed there anyway because the shock interacts with the boundary
layer. The nose wave drag is therefore modelled as a smooth (cubic) rise from zero at $M=0.8$ to
the computed $M=1.1$ value at $M=1.0$, flat up to $M=1.1$. This assumption is common to all
shapes. The transonic Fluent runs listed in Section~\ref{sec:next} replace it. Near $M = 1$,
local supersonic pockets on the body end in shocks that thicken the boundary layer and can
separate it, so viscous effects change the pressure field itself and the Euler model no longer
applies. RANS (Reynolds-averaged Navier--Stokes) equations include the boundary layer with a
turbulence model. The start of the rise at $M = 0.8$ corresponds to the first appearance of sonic
flow on a slender nose. Because this model is the least certain part of the chain, its onset and
amplitude are varied in the robustness study (Section~\ref{sec:robust}).

\subsection{Flight model}
\label{sec:flight}

A point-mass trajectory is integrated from the \SI{12}{m} rail ($84^\circ$) to apogee. The
integration is done in Cartesian coordinates; on the rail the direction is fixed, and after rail
exit thrust and drag act along the velocity. Written along the flight path, with $V_\infty$ the
speed and $\gamma_f$ the flight-path angle above the horizontal, the equations of motion are
\begin{equation}
m\frac{\mathrm dV_\infty}{\mathrm dt} = F_{\rm th} - \tfrac12\rho V_\infty^2 A_b C_D - m g\sin\gamma_f ,
\qquad
V_\infty\frac{\mathrm d\gamma_f}{\mathrm dt} = -g\cos\gamma_f ,
\label{eq:eom}
\end{equation}
with the mass $m$ decreasing linearly during the burn and the air density $\rho$ from the ISA at
the current altitude. The first equation is Newton's second law along the velocity; the second
describes the gravity turn, i.e.\ the slow tilting of the trajectory under gravity. The drag
coefficient is built up from components (reference area $\pi D^2/4$):
\begin{equation}
C_D = k\left(C_{D,f} + C_{D,\rm fins} + C_{D,\rm base} + C_{D,e}\right) + C_{D,\rm w}(M),
\label{eq:buildup}
\end{equation}
with turbulent, roughness-limited skin friction on the wetted area of nose and body (Schlichting,
compressibility-corrected), fin pressure drag (blunt leading edge subsonic, Ackeret hexagon
supersonic), the OpenRocket base-drag law (nozzle exit area removed while burning) and the ERT-Sim
excrescence law. The single factor $k$ is set so that the baseline nose (Von K\'arm\'an,
\SI{720}{mm}) reaches the LVO apogee of \SI{9.0}{km}. The resulting $M_{\max}=1.47$ is close to
the LVO's Mach 1.4 without further tuning. A longer nose adds its length to the vehicle and its
extra shell mass to the vehicle mass.

The terms of Equation~\eqref{eq:buildup} are the standard components of rocket drag estimation:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
\item $C_{D,f}$, \emph{skin friction}: the shear stress of the turbulent boundary layer, proportional
to the wetted area. It is computed from a flat-plate correlation as a function of the Reynolds
number. When the viscous sublayer becomes thinner than the surface roughness, the friction
coefficient stops decreasing with Reynolds number (``roughness-limited''). A correction accounts
for the lower density in the hot boundary layer at high Mach number.
\item $C_{D,\rm fins}$, \emph{fin pressure drag}: from the blunt fin leading edges below Mach 1, and
from linearised supersonic thin-airfoil theory (Ackeret) for the hexagonal section above it.
\item $C_{D,\rm base}$, \emph{base drag}: the low pressure in the wake behind the flat base. While the
motor burns, the jet fills the nozzle exit area, which is then removed from the base area.
\item $C_{D,e}$, \emph{excrescences}: rail buttons, fasteners, joints and other protrusions, with the
law of the ERT simulator.
\end{itemize}
Only $C_{D,\rm w}$ changes from one nose to the next. The length and mass effects of a longer nose
enter through the wetted area in $C_{D,f}$ and through $m$.

\subsection{Heating}
\label{sec:heating_method}

The wall cannot exceed the stagnation temperature $T_0=T_\infty(1+\tfrac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2)$, which
is the Firehorn design rule; the transient below shows how close it gets. $T_0$ follows from the
steady energy equation $c_pT + \tfrac12V^2 = \text{const}$ along a streamline: it is the temperature
air reaches when it is brought to rest without heat exchange, all its kinetic energy being
converted into enthalpy. For example, on the hot day ($T_\infty \approx \SI{288}{K}$ at
\SI{2.3}{km}) at $M = 1.56$, $T_0 = 288\times(1+0.2\times1.56^2) = \SI{428}{K}$ (\SI{155}{\celsius}).
How close a surface gets to this bound depends on the balance between the heat it receives,
$q = h\,(T_r - T_w)$, and the heat it conducts away into the material. At the tip, the laminar
stagnation-point coefficient (Fay--Riddell form without dissociation) with the modified-Newtonian
velocity gradient is
\begin{equation}
h_s = 0.763\,\mathrm{Pr}^{-0.6}\sqrt{\rho_s\mu_s \left.\frac{\mathrm du_e}{\mathrm dx}\right|_s}\;c_p,
\qquad
\left.\frac{\mathrm du_e}{\mathrm dx}\right|_s = \frac{1}{r_n}\sqrt{\frac{2(p_s-p_\infty)}{\rho_s}} ,
\label{eq:hs}
\end{equation}
so $h_s \propto r_n^{-1/2}$. Here $\rho_s$, $\mu_s$ and $p_s$ are the density, viscosity and pressure
at the stagnation point (behind the normal part of the bow shock), $c_p$ the specific heat of air
and Pr its Prandtl number. $\mathrm du_e/\mathrm dx|_s$ is the rate at which the flow at the edge of
the boundary layer accelerates away from the stagnation point; the modified-Newtonian estimate
gives it from the pressure difference and the tip radius. A larger tip radius gives a gentler
acceleration and a thicker boundary layer, hence a lower heat-transfer coefficient. This is why
re-entry vehicles have blunt noses. Along the shell, the turbulent flat-plate correlation
$\mathrm{St}=0.0296\,\mathrm{Re}_x^{*-0.2}\mathrm{Pr}^{-2/3}$ (Eckert reference temperature) drives
the recovery temperature $T_r = T_\infty(1+r\tfrac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2)$, $r=\mathrm{Pr}^{1/3}$. The
Stanton number $\mathrm{St} = h/(\rho u c_p)$ is the dimensionless heat-transfer coefficient, and
$\mathrm{Re}_x$ the Reynolds number on the distance $x$ from the tip. The asterisk means that the
fluid properties are evaluated at Eckert's reference temperature, a weighted mean of the edge,
wall and recovery temperatures that accounts for the variation of properties across a hot
boundary layer. The recovery temperature is the temperature of an insulated wall under a boundary
layer. It lies slightly below $T_0$ (recovery factor $r \approx 0.89$) because part of the
dissipated energy is conducted away from the wall within the boundary layer. The
wall response is a 1D transient conduction problem with an adiabatic inner face, which is
conservative because it neglects radiation and internal convection:
\begin{equation}
\rho_m c_m\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = k_m\frac{\partial^2 T}{\partial y^2},
\qquad
-k_m\left.\frac{\partial T}{\partial y}\right|_{\rm outer} = h\,(T_r - T_w),
\qquad
\left.\frac{\partial T}{\partial y}\right|_{\rm inner} = 0 ,
\label{eq:conduction}
\end{equation}
with $\rho_m$, $c_m$, $k_m$ the density, specific heat and conductivity of the shell material and $y$
the coordinate through the thickness. Both are evaluated along the
nominal and the hot-day envelope trajectories. A metal tip conducts heat so well that its
temperature is nearly uniform; it is modelled as a single lumped mass heated through its wetted
surface.

\subsection{Stability, mass and payload}

Barrowman's method gives the nose $C_{N\alpha}=2$ at $x_{cp}=L-V/A_b$ and the fin contribution
($C_{N\alpha,f}=7.4$ for the assumed fins). The change in static margin relative to the baseline
nose is computed with positions measured from the aft end, including the CG shift from the
nose-shell mass. The shell mass is the wetted area times the Firehorn as-built areal density
(\SI{0.98}{kg/m^2}: \SI{500}{g} measured for the \SI{960}{mm}, \O\SI{240}{mm} nose). The 3U fit is
the length of curved nose over which $r\geq\SI{77}{mm}$. Barrowman's method is the standard
linear (small angle of attack, subsonic) method of model rocketry, also used by OpenRocket. It
adds the normal-force slopes of the components and places the vehicle CP at their weighted mean,
\begin{equation}
x_{cp} = \frac{\sum_i C_{N\alpha,i}\,x_{cp,i}}{\sum_i C_{N\alpha,i}}, \qquad
\text{static margin} = \frac{x_{cp} - x_{cg}}{D} ,
\label{eq:barrowman}
\end{equation}
with positions measured from the apex. Measuring from the aft end instead keeps the fins and the
body fixed when the nose length changes.

\subsection{Design catalogue, robustness and CAD}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Direct runs at the design fineness.} All seven shapes (and the blunted Von K\'arm\'an
nose) were also computed at $L/D = 4.5$ for $M = 1.6$, 1.4 and 1.2. The full flow fields were saved
for the pressure maps of Section~\ref{sec:catalogue}.
\item \textbf{Robustness.} The apogee of every shape at $L/D = 4.5$ is recomputed with the onset of
the transonic drag rise moved to $M = 0.75$ and 0.85, the transonic wave drag scaled by
$\pm20\,\%$, the rest of the vehicle drag increased by 20\,\%, and the hot-day envelope with the LVO
masses. These perturbations target the least certain inputs: the transonic model, the
calibration factor $k$ and the mass model.
\item \textbf{Renders and CAD.} The shaded views of Figure~\ref{fig:gallery} are computed from the
exact profiles (Lambert--Phong shading of the surface of revolution). The STL solids are
triangulated surfaces of revolution (128 azimuthal facets) closed by a flat base. Their
closure and outward normals were checked, and their volume matches the analytical one.
\end{itemize}
