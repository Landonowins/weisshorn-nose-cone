\section{Selection}
\label{sec:selection}

\subsection{Profile}

Table~\ref{tab:matrix} scores the profiles at $L/D = 4.5$ on the criteria of
Section~\ref{sec:options}. Heating is left out because, with a metallic tip, it does not depend
on the profile (Section~\ref{sec:heating}).

The matrix is a weighted-sum decision method. Each design receives a score $s_i$ from 1 (worst)
to 5 (best) on each criterion $i$, and its total is $\sum_i w_i s_i$ with weights $w_i$ that add
up to one. The quantitative criteria are scored linearly between the worst and the best design:
performance on the apogee change $\Delta z$, integration on the length of 3U inside the curved
part, and stability on the change in static margin. Manufacture and heritage are judgement
scores. For manufacture, a tangent shoulder and a standard mould score 5, a shoulder kink is
penalised, and the cone gets 3 for its simple, developable mould. For heritage, the profile of the
current Firehorn vehicle scores 5, the ogive of earlier vehicles 4, and profiles without ERT
flight history lower. Performance carries the largest weight (0.4) because apogee margin is what
the nose shape mainly affects and what systems engineering trades against mass growth.
Integration and manufacture (0.2 each) decide whether the design can be housed and built.
Heritage and stability (0.1 each) matter less: heritage lowers risk without changing the
hardware, and the static margin varies by less than 0.1 cal between candidates.

\textbf{Von K\'arm\'an is selected.} Three profiles form a leading group on flight performance:
Von K\'arm\'an, power $\tfrac12$ and power $\tfrac34$. At the design length, Von K\'arm\'an is
first. It has the lowest wave drag at every computed Mach number, flies \SI{18}{m} higher than
power $\tfrac34$ and \SI{22}{m} higher than power $\tfrac12$, and stays first in all seven robustness
scenarios (Figure~\ref{fig:robustness}). Ogive, parabolic and LV-Haack lose \SIrange{69}{79}{m},
and the cone \SI{132}{m}. Von K\'arm\'an also wins on the other criteria:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item it is tangent to the body at the shoulder, so there is no kink at the coupler bond. The
power-$\tfrac12$ nose has a $3.2^\circ$ kink and the power-$\tfrac34$ a $4.8^\circ$ kink at
$L/D=4.5$;
\item it holds more of the 3U unit (\FINTHREEU{} inside the curved part, against
\SI{217}{mm} for power $\tfrac12$ and \SI{152}{mm} for power $\tfrac34$);
\item its advantage holds at $M \geq 1.2$, so it does not hinge on the transonic model.
\end{itemize}
The Firehorn power-$\tfrac12$ nose remains an acceptable alternative (\SI{22}{m} lower) if
reusing its tooling outweighs the shoulder kink (R10, Section~\ref{sec:recommendations}).

The outcome depends on the weights in one case only. If the weights of performance and
integration were swapped (payload room 0.4, performance 0.2), the tangent ogive would score
higher than Von K\'arm\'an (3.98 against 3.89). The selection therefore rests on the premise that
apogee margin matters more than payload room, which holds as long as the payload is a single 3U
unit that the Von K\'arm\'an nose can house. If the payload grows, the volume-driven alternatives
of Table~\ref{tab:alternatives} apply.

\subsection{Length}

Every \SI{90}{mm} of nose ($\Delta L/D = 0.5$) around $L/D = 4$--4.5 is worth \TRADELEN{} of
apogee, about \SI{0.4}{kg} of mass margin at \SI{-143}{m/kg}. It also costs about \SI{60}{mm} of
nose-bay length (the extra length is partly recovered because more of the 3U fits inside the
curved part) and about \SI{30}{g} of shell. With the LV capped at \SI{5.4}{m}
(2027\_C\_SE\_REQ\_04) and dry-mass growth expected (the LVS flags its own mass as
underestimated; 2027\_C\_SE\_REQ\_06 caps it at \SI{50}{kg}), the design point is
\textbf{$L/D = 4.5$, $L = \SI{810}{mm}$}, which gives \TRADELEN{} of margin for \SI{62}{mm} of
bay length. If systems engineering cannot release those \SI{62}{mm}, the same profile at
$L/D = 4$ (\SI{720}{mm}, the LVS length) is the fallback; nothing else in the design changes.

The trade-off behind this choice is between apogee and vehicle length. The wave drag falls as
$1/(L/D)^2$, so each extra \SI{90}{mm} is worth less than the previous one, while the length,
wetted area and mass it adds grow linearly. A further step to $L/D = 5$ would still add about
\SI{39}{m} (Table~\ref{tab:catalogue}) for about \SI{60}{mm} more bay. $L/D = 4.5$ is the step
requested here because every millimetre comes out of a \SI{5.4}{m} budget that is already at its
cap. The same reasoning can be extended if systems engineering finds more length.

\subsection{Tip}

A spherical tip of radius $r_n = \SI{4.5}{mm}$ ($r_n/R = 0.05$), machined in Al 6082-T6 as a
replaceable insert:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item it removes the sharp apex that cannot be laid up in prepreg and chips on landing;
\item it costs \BLUNTCOST{} in wave drag (\BLUNTAPOA{} of apogee). A \SI{9}{mm} tip would lower the
stagnation heating by $\sqrt2$ but cost \BLUNTAPOB{}, and the aluminium tip does not need it;
\item it stays below \TIPAL{} in the envelope case, whereas a printed PET-CF tip reaches
\PETENV{};
\item it adds about \SI{60}{g} at the very front, which helps the static margin.
\end{itemize}
The radius is a compromise between drag and heating. At $r_n/R = 0.05$ the drag cost is about
1\,\% or less over the Mach range, whereas $r_n/R = 0.1$ already costs about 4\,\%
(Figure~\ref{fig:bluntness}a). A smaller radius would raise the stagnation heating
($h_s \propto r_n^{-1/2}$) and make the tip more fragile to machine and handle. Because the
aluminium insert keeps the tip cool at this radius, nothing is gained by blunting it further.
