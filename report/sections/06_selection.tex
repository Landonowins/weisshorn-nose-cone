\section{Selection}
\label{sec:selection}

\subsection{Profile}

Table~\ref{tab:matrix} scores the profiles at $L/D = 4.5$ from 1 (worst) to 5 (best) and sums the
scores with weights in the ratio 4:2:2:1. Performance (apogee change), integration (3U length inside
the curved part) and stability (static-margin change) are scored linearly between the worst and the
best design; manufacture is a judgement score (a tangent shoulder and a standard mould score 5, a
kink is penalised, the cone gets 3 for its simple mould). Performance carries the largest weight
(0.44) because apogee margin is what the nose shape mainly affects; integration and manufacture
(0.22 each) decide whether the design can be housed and built; stability (0.11) matters least,
since the margin varies by less than 0.1 cal. Heating is left out because, with a metallic tip, it
does not depend on the profile. Only properties of the hardware are scored: whether a profile has
flown on an earlier ERT vehicle is not a criterion.

\textbf{Von K\'arm\'an is selected.} It has the lowest wave drag at every computed Mach number,
flies \SI{18}{m} higher than power $\tfrac34$ and \SI{22}{m} higher than power $\tfrac12$, and stays
first in all seven robustness scenarios; ogive, parabolic and LV-Haack lose \SIrange{69}{79}{m}, and
the cone \SI{132}{m}. Against the two power noses it also wins on integration: a tangent shoulder
(no kink at the coupler bond, against $3.2^\circ$ and $4.8^\circ$) and more of the 3U inside
(\FINTHREEU{}, against \SI{217}{mm} and \SI{152}{mm}). Its advantage holds at $M \geq 1.2$, so it
does not hinge on the transonic model.

The outcome does not depend on the exact weights. With equal weights Von K\'arm\'an still leads
LV-Haack (3.95 against 3.48); with performance and integration swapped it still scores highest,
level with LV-Haack (4.10 against 4.09). It loses first place, to LV-Haack, only if payload room
carries more than about half of the total weight, i.e.\ if the payload grows beyond a single 3U unit
(Table~\ref{tab:alternatives}).

\subsection{Length}

Every \SI{90}{mm} of nose ($\Delta L/D = 0.5$) around $L/D = 4$--4.5 is worth \TRADELEN{} of
apogee, about \SI{0.4}{kg} of mass margin, for about \SI{60}{mm} of nose-bay length (partly
recovered because more of the 3U fits inside) and \SI{30}{g} of shell. The returns diminish: the
wave drag falls as $1/(L/D)^2$ while length, wetted area and mass grow linearly, and a further step
to $L/D = 5$ would add only about \SI{39}{m}. With the LV capped at \SI{5.4}{m}
(2027\_C\_SE\_REQ\_04) and dry-mass growth expected (2027\_C\_SE\_REQ\_06 caps it at \SI{50}{kg}),
the design point is \textbf{$L/D = 4.5$, $L = \SI{810}{mm}$}, which gives \TRADELEN{} of margin for
\SI{62}{mm} of bay length. If systems engineering cannot release those \SI{62}{mm}, the same profile
at $L/D = 4$ (\SI{720}{mm}, the LVS length) is the fallback; nothing else changes.

\subsection{Tip}

A spherical tip of radius $r_n = \SI{4.5}{mm}$ ($r_n/R = 0.05$), machined in Al 6082-T6 as a
replaceable insert. It removes the sharp apex that cannot be laid up and chips on landing, costs
\BLUNTCOST{} in wave drag (\BLUNTAPOA{} of apogee), stays below \TIPAL{} in the envelope case
(against \PETENV{} for a printed PET-CF tip), and adds about \SI{60}{g} at the very front, which helps
the static margin. A \SI{9}{mm} tip would lower the stagnation heating by only $\sqrt2$ for
\BLUNTAPOB{}, and the aluminium insert does not need it.
